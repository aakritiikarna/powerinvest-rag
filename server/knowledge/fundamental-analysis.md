# Fundamental Analysis

## What fundamental analysis is
Fundamental analysis is a method of evaluating a company by studying its business and financial health in order to estimate its intrinsic value, meaning what the company is worth based on its underlying performance. The investor then compares that estimate with the current market price to judge whether the stock looks undervalued, fairly valued or overvalued. It is mainly used by longer-term investors.

## Financial statements
Three statements form the core of the analysis. The income statement shows revenue, expenses and profit over a period. The balance sheet shows what the company owns (assets), what it owes (liabilities) and the shareholders' equity at a point in time. The cash flow statement shows how cash moves through operations, investing and financing. Reading all three gives a more complete picture than profit alone.

## Profitability and growth measures
Earnings per share (EPS) is net profit divided by the number of shares outstanding. Revenue growth shows whether sales are expanding. Profit margins, such as gross margin and net margin, show how much of each dollar of sales turns into profit. Return on equity (ROE) measures how efficiently a company uses shareholder money to generate profit. Consistent growth and healthy margins are generally positive signs.

## Valuation ratios
The price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio divides the share price by earnings per share and shows how much investors pay for each dollar of profit. The price-to-book (P/B) ratio compares price with book value, and the price-to-sales ratio compares price with revenue. Ratios are most useful when compared with the company's own history and with similar companies in the same industry, not in isolation.

## Financial strength
The debt-to-equity ratio compares total debt with shareholders' equity and indicates how much the company relies on borrowing. A company with heavy debt may struggle when earnings fall or interest rates rise. The current ratio compares short-term assets with short-term liabilities and indicates whether the company can pay its near-term bills. Free cash flow, the cash left after capital spending, shows whether the business generates real cash.

## Qualitative factors
Numbers do not tell the whole story. Analysts also look at the business model, competitive advantages (sometimes called a moat), quality of management, brand strength, industry trends, regulation and the overall economy. A company with strong financials but a declining industry or weak leadership may still be a poor investment.

## Limits of fundamental analysis
Estimates of intrinsic value depend on assumptions about the future, which can be wrong. A stock that looks cheap can stay cheap or become cheaper, and the market can take a long time to recognize value. Financial statements are also based on accounting choices and past results. Fundamental analysis improves judgment but does not guarantee outcomes.
