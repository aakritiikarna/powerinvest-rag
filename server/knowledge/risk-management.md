# Risk Management in Stock Investing

## What investment risk means
Investment risk is the possibility that an investment's actual return will differ from what you expected, including the possibility of losing money. Risk cannot be eliminated, because returns come from accepting uncertainty. Risk management is the practice of understanding the risks you take and keeping them at a level you can live with, rather than avoiding risk entirely.

## Main types of risk
Market risk is the chance that the whole market falls and drags most stocks down. Company-specific risk comes from events affecting one business, such as poor earnings or a scandal. Sector risk affects a whole industry, such as new regulation. Inflation risk is the loss of purchasing power over time. Liquidity risk is the difficulty of selling an asset quickly at a fair price. Concentration risk arises when too much money sits in one stock or theme.

## Know your risk tolerance
Risk tolerance is how much loss you can accept, financially and emotionally, without abandoning your plan. Risk capacity is how much loss you can afford given your income, savings and time horizon. A sound plan respects both. An investor who panics and sells during a downturn often locks in losses, so choosing a level of risk you can stick with is part of risk management.

## Position sizing
Position sizing means deciding how much of your portfolio to place in any single investment. Limiting each position so that a bad outcome in one stock cannot seriously damage the whole portfolio is one of the most practical risk controls. Some investors set a maximum percentage per stock, for instance a small single-digit or low double-digit share of the portfolio.

## Stop-loss orders and exit rules
A stop-loss order is an instruction to sell a stock automatically if its price falls to a chosen level, aiming to limit a loss. It can help enforce discipline, but it has limits: in a fast-falling market the sale may execute below the chosen price, and a short-term dip can trigger a sale of a stock that later recovers. Deciding in advance when you would exit a position helps avoid emotional decisions.

## Diversification and rebalancing
Spreading money across different companies, sectors and asset classes reduces the impact of any single failure. Over time, winners grow and the portfolio drifts away from its target mix, so periodic rebalancing, which means selling some of what has grown and buying what has lagged, restores the intended level of risk.

## Understanding risk and reward
Higher potential returns usually come with higher risk. Before investing, compare what you could gain with what you could lose. Concepts such as maximum drawdown, which is the largest peak-to-trough decline an investment has experienced, help show how painful past declines were. Borrowing money to invest, known as leverage, magnifies both gains and losses and can add serious risk.
