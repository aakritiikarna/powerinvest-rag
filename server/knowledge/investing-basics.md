# Investing Basics

## Investing versus saving
Saving means setting money aside in low-risk places such as a savings account, where the goal is safety and easy access. Investing means putting money into assets such as stocks, bonds or funds with the aim of growing it over time, accepting that the value can fluctuate and that you may lose some of what you invest. Money you may need within a few years generally belongs in savings rather than in stocks.

## Set goals and a time horizon
Before investing, define what the money is for and when you will need it. A goal that is decades away, such as retirement, can usually tolerate more short-term ups and downs than a goal that is only a few years away. Your time horizon and your comfort with losses together shape how much of your portfolio should be in higher-risk assets.

## The power of compounding
Compounding means earning returns on your earlier returns, not only on your original amount. Over long periods this effect can become large, which is why starting early and staying invested matters. For example, returns that are reinvested produce their own returns in later years. Compounding works in reverse for fees and debt, which also build up over time.

## Main asset classes
Stocks represent ownership in companies and have historically offered higher long-term growth with higher volatility. Bonds are loans to governments or companies that pay interest and are generally less volatile than stocks. Cash and cash equivalents are the safest but offer the lowest long-term growth. Other assets such as real estate and commodities can also play a role. Each class behaves differently in different economic conditions.

## Funds, ETFs and index funds
A mutual fund or exchange-traded fund (ETF) pools money from many investors to buy a basket of securities. An index fund aims to match the performance of a market index rather than beat it, usually with low fees. Funds let a beginner own many companies at once without choosing individual stocks, which provides built-in diversification.

## Dollar-cost averaging
Dollar-cost averaging means investing a fixed amount at regular intervals, such as monthly, regardless of the current price. You buy more shares when prices are low and fewer when prices are high. It does not guarantee a profit or protect against losses, but it reduces the pressure of trying to pick the perfect moment to invest.

## Costs matter
Fees such as fund expense ratios, trading commissions and taxes reduce your net return, and the effect grows over many years. When two investments are otherwise similar, the lower-cost option keeps more of the return for the investor. Reviewing costs before investing is a simple habit with a lasting impact.

## Build a safety net first
Many people keep an emergency fund covering several months of essential expenses before investing, so they are not forced to sell investments at a bad time to cover an unexpected bill. Paying off high-interest debt is also often a priority, because the interest owed can exceed typical investment returns.
